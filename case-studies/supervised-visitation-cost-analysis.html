Case study · Indiana Department of Child Services · 2025

Where $320 million in supervised visitation went, and what it bought

Supervised visitation is one of the largest service lines in Indiana’s child welfare budget. Leadership asked how the service is actually used, how decisions about intensity are made, and what other states do differently. I answered with five years of administrative data and a national scan.

My roleLead analyst and author
OrganizationIndiana Department of Child Services, Research & Evaluation
DeliveredJanuary 2025, to agency leadership
ToolsSQL, R, Excel
$320Min supervised visitation spending analyzed, January 2019 to September 2024

01

The challenge

The agency was spending roughly $62 million a year on supervised visitation, but nobody could say with confidence how that money translated into services for families.

Leadership wanted to know who receives supervised visitation, at what level of supervision and for how long; whether families actually progress toward less restrictive visits, as the agency’s own service standard expects; whether the service is delivered equitably; and how other states keep costs down without compromising child safety.

02

My approach

  1. Grounded the questions in the evidence baseReviewed the research on best practices, structured decision-making frameworks, national court guidelines and the federal laws and grants that shape the service.
  2. Scanned what other states doCompared cost-saving and eligibility models in Washington, Texas, Minnesota, California and Utah, from volunteer supervision to flexible scheduling and group visits.
  3. Talked to the fieldGathered practice guidance from Indiana’s association of residential child care agencies on how supervision levels are assigned.
  4. Analyzed five years of administrative dataUsed SQL and R to trace spending, visit volume, supervision levels, provider concentration and each case’s trajectory, splitting case durations into equal intervals to test whether visit frequency and supervision changed over time.
  5. Looked at equityCompared visit patterns by parent gender and by race against each group’s share of substantiated assessments.
03

What I found

+76%Visits, 2020–2023

Demand grew while spending stayed flat

Annual spending held near $62 million while total visits climbed from 2.4 million to 4.2 million, so far more visits were being delivered for each dollar than before.

0%Of cases stepped down

Supervision almost never decreased

The service standard expects supervision to ease as families progress. Of 20,211 cases with complete data, 59% saw no change in supervision level and 41% moved to more intensive supervision. None moved to less.

33%Of services, two providers

The market was highly concentrated

Two contracted providers delivered about a third of all services between them; the next largest delivered just 3%.

4×Mixed-race share of visits

Visit volume was not evenly distributed

Families identified as mixed race made up 9% of substantiated assessments but received 41% of visits, while Black families made up 18% of assessments and received 12% of visits. The data could not show why, which is itself a finding.

Indiana’s eligibility criteria cover every substantiated case. Other states reserve supervised visitation for clear, ongoing risk, which is where most of their savings come from.

Key takeaway
04

What I recommended

Target eligibility to riskConsider narrowing referral criteria toward clear, ongoing risk factors such as domestic violence or severe substance use, while keeping flexibility for other cases.
Borrow proven cost modelsCommunity-based and volunteer supervision (Texas, Utah), flexible scheduling and group visits (Minnesota) and performance-based contracting (Washington).
Fix the data before judging the practiceReferral reasons live in PDFs and free-text fields. A short qualitative study is needed before the agency can tie safety scores to visit dosage or explain equity gaps.
Monitor step-downsTrack whether supervision decreases as the standard requires, so cases do not stay at the most intensive (and most expensive) level by default.
