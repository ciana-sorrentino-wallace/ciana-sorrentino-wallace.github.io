Case study · Indiana Department of Child Services · 2025

A routine analysis uncovered 140,000 duplicate hotline reports

While pulling numbers for the hotline team, I noticed the official report and the case management system disagreed by more than 140,000 reports. I traced the gap to a single column in a source table and quantified exactly how much it had inflated a year of reporting.

ToolsSQL, Excel

01

The challenge

Hotline volume drives staffing, budgets and what the agency tells legislators and the public. If the count is wrong, every decision built on it is too.

The agency’s reporting system produces a daily hotline decision summary that rolls up into monthly and annual figures. While preparing an analysis for the hotline team, I found its yearly total did not match the number of reports I could pull directly from the case management system, and the difference was far too large to be rounding or timing.

02

My approach

  1. Reconciled the totals four waysPulled counts from the case management system and from the source table behind the official report, each on a distinct and a non-distinct basis, by created date and by report date.
  2. Found the matching numberThe non-distinct source-table total (354,675) was the only figure close to the official report (355,609), which pointed to the source table as the cause.
  3. Isolated the defectSampled records and found each report identifier appearing twice, with the download date the only field that differed. The table was capturing the same report on consecutive download days.
  4. Measured the damageCounted every affected report and traced how far back the pattern went: it had been present since the April 2022 to April 2023 reporting year.
  5. Wrote it up for actionA four-page report with the reconciliation tables, the evidence sample and the correction needed to restore accurate counts.
03

Identifiers are surrogate values. The pattern is real: same report, same data, different download date.

65%Of distinct reports

Nearly two in three reports were counted twice

140,114 of roughly 214,500 distinct reports appeared twice in the source table, producing 280,228 records where there should have been 140,114.

39.5%Of all records

Over a third of the reported volume was not real

Of the 354,675 source-table records, 39.5% were duplicates. Only 60.5% represented actual, distinct reports.

2 yearsAffected

The problem predated the analysis

The duplication was present in the prior reporting year too, so earlier published figures were likely affected as well.

The numbers matched closely enough to look trustworthy and differed enough to matter. The only way to see it was to reconcile the same count from every direction.

Key takeaway
04

Why it mattered

Overstated hotline volume can justify the wrong staffing levels, distort trend lines shown to leadership and the legislature, and undermine trust in every other figure from the same system. Finding the defect at the column level made the correction precise: count each report once, regardless of how many times it was downloaded.

This is the same instinct I brought to validating federal AFCARS reports as the agency’s designated data champion during its case management system consolidation: check the number from more than one direction before anyone builds on it.
